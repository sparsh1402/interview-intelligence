## How to structure any answer (STAR, ~2 minutes)

| Part | Share of time | What it must contain |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | ~15% | Team, system, stakes, in one or two sentences |
| **Task** | ~10% | What *you* owned |
| **Action** | ~55% | What *you* did, step by step, with the reasoning ("I" not "we") |
| **Result** | ~20% | Measurable outcome + what you learned / would do differently |

Common failures: a long Situation, "we" everywhere, no numbers, no reflection, stories where the other person is the villain.

Keep a **story bank** of 5–6 stories that each cover several themes (conflict, ambiguity, ownership, mentoring, failure, impact), so one story can answer several questions.

## Q-0028 — Handling a disagreement

**What they're testing:** you disagree with data and respect, listen, can commit to a decision you lost, and keep the relationship.

**Pick a story where:** a technical disagreement (design choice, tech debt vs feature, code review dispute) with a peer, senior or another team.

**Action beats to cover:**
1. You first understood their reasoning (asked questions, restated their view).
2. You brought evidence: a benchmark, prototype, data, user impact.
3. You found shared goals or a compromise, or escalated cleanly with both options written down.
4. The outcome, and **disagree-and-commit** if you didn't win.

**Avoid:** stories where you were simply right and they were wrong; personal conflicts.

**Follow-ups to prepare:** "What if they still disagreed?" "Have you ever been wrong in such a disagreement?" "How did the relationship end up?"

## Q-0029 — A decision made with incomplete information

**What they're testing:** comfort with ambiguity, reversible vs irreversible decisions, managing risk.

**Action beats:**
1. What was unknown and why you couldn't wait (deadline, incident, customer).
2. How you bounded the risk: gathered the cheapest available data, made assumptions explicit, picked a reversible option, added monitoring / feature flag / rollback.
3. Who you informed and how.
4. What happened, and how you validated or corrected the decision later.

**Good sources in backend work:** a production incident, choosing a library or schema under a deadline, capacity planning before real traffic.

**Follow-ups:** "What would have made you wait?" "What did you get wrong?"

## Q-0030 — Mentoring junior engineers

**What they're testing (especially at SDE-2):** that you multiply the team's output, not just your own.

**Action beats:**
1. Who you mentored and what their gap was.
2. What you did concretely: pairing, code-review style, design walkthroughs, giving them ownership of a scoped task with a safety net.
3. How you adapted to them (didn't just hand over answers).
4. Result for them (shipped X independently, ramp-up time) and for the team.

**If you haven't formally mentored:** use onboarding a new joiner, leading a knowledge-sharing session, or reviewing an intern's work. Be honest about the scale.

**Follow-ups:** "A mentee who wasn't improving?" "How do you give critical feedback?"

## Q-0031 — Background / self-introduction

**Format (60–90 seconds):** present → past → why this role.
1. **Now:** role, team, the system you own and its scale (in one line each).
2. **Highlights:** 2 achievements with numbers that you can defend under follow-up (see Mastercard Q-0018 — every number will be probed).
3. **Why here:** what this role adds to your trajectory.

Don't recite the resume chronologically; pick the 2 things you most want them to ask about.
